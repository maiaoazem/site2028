A practice-based protocol designed to protect human critical and creative abilities in a new era when thinking can increasingly be outsourced to machines.

(01)

the club for organizations

Weekly sessions combining reading, dialogue, and writing original contexts for enterprise AI. Human development and better interactions with technology, through the same practice.

120 minutes per week · 5 stages · groups of up to 15

  1. (01)20 min

    Check-in

    Check-in to begin: 20 minutes to share how AI is being used and what questions are emerging at work and in everyday life.

  2. (02)25 min

    Reading

    Reading in depth, individually, for 25 minutes, annotating books, highlighting passages, and outlining context in personal notes.

    Paper Has an Edge over Screens

    • A meta-analysis of 54 studies involving 171,055 participants found a small but statistically significant advantage for paper over screens (g ≈ 0,21). (Delgado et al., 2018)
  3. (03)45 min

    Discussion

    Discussion and synthesis of the readings for 45 minutes, agreeing on at least one shared context.

    Discuss and Build

    • Discussion formats grounded in open questions, where participants present arguments, examine contradictions, and justify their interpretations, can improve aspects of comprehension (Graham and Hebert, 2011)
    • Additional research identified more sophisticated reasoning in critical-analytical discussions, especially with authentic questions and responses that build on earlier contributions. (Soter et al., 2008)
  4. (04)20 min

    Writing

    Writing by hand for 20 minutes, producing a group-authored text to serve as context for the AI system.

    Writing Makes Learning Stick

    • Writing about what was read improved comprehension compared with traditional reading activities, including rereading and discussion. (Graham and Hebert, 2011)
  5. (05)10 min

    Context

    Context for the machine: 10 minutes with AI assistance to create a .md file that can be tested throughout the week.

    contexts/week.md

Groups of up to 15 people, with monthly assessments of perceived human development, at the individual and collective levels, and of the AI systems'critical and creative capabilities.

(01)AttentionOvercoming Cognitive Passivity

Overcoming Cognitive Passivity

Attention was already under pressure as information consumption changed in the internet and mobile eras. The rise of AI may intensify this trend. Attention—the foundation of critical thinking—could be at even greater risk.

10 minof AI assistance can already make complex tasks harder to perform independently

Researchers are investigating whether outsourcing thinking can have lingering effects even after brief interactions with AI.

In randomized experiments involving 1,222 participants, researchers found that roughly ten minutes of AI assistance could reduce persistence when facing problems and impair subsequent performance without the tool. AI helped participants solve exercises in the moment, but they were less likely to keep trying when they had to work independently.

(Liu et al., 2026)

2010the beginning of declines in reading and reasoning, with young people reporting difficulty thinking

The ability to sustain attention was showing signs of strain before generative AI became widespread.

An analysis by the Financial Times, drawing on international assessments and longitudinal research, identified declines in reading and reasoning performance starting in the early 2010s, as well as an increase from the middle of that decade in the share of young people reporting difficulty thinking, concentrating, and learning. The analysis links these trends to shifts in information consumption, particularly from active exploration toward passive consumption of digital content.

(Burn-Murdoch, 2025)

(02)InterpretationReading Is a Culturally Learned Skill

Reading Is a Culturally Learned Skill

While spoken language is acquired spontaneously, reading requires deliberate instruction and cultural reinforcement. Our brains have not evolved specifically to read. Instead, they repurpose part of the visual cortex, which becomes specialized in letters: the “visual word form area,” what Dehaene calls the brain's “letterbox.” The system that lets us recognize an apple across different shapes also helps us recognize an A or a B in different fonts.

32%slower cognitive decline among people who read and write frequently

Reading, writing, and interpreting information engage our cognitive abilities, helping exercise and maintain the processes involved in thinking.

A study following 294 older adults through annual assessments and postmortem brain examinations found that frequent participation in intellectually stimulating activities, including reading and writing, was associated with a 32% slower rate of cognitive decline. Among participants with little cognitive activity, decline was 48% faster than among those with average activity levels.

(Wilson et al., 2013)

(03)Interdisciplinary ThinkingSystems Thinking

Systems Thinking

Because context is not drawn from a single book but emerges from the encounter between different readings and people, both individual and collective thinking can become more open to understanding the complex systems we inhabit and the relationships within them.

55studies suggest that crossing disciplines supports creativity

Combining knowledge from different disciplines can improve our ability to develop original ideas and interpretations.

A meta-analysis of 55 studies involving 7,490 students found significant positive effects of interdisciplinary teaching on creativity, particularly creative thinking processes and the ability to apply new ideas. The researchers identify knowledge recombination, integration of perspectives, and transfer of ideas across contexts as possible mechanisms behind these benefits.

(Chi et al., 2026)

(04)Critical ThinkingThe Capacity to Question

The Capacity to Question

Business consultancies are increasingly examining what happens when analytical reasoning and decision-making are outsourced to algorithms: immediate productivity gains may gradually erode an organization's human ROI and strategic capacity.

de-skillingacross the organization: the collective erosion of critical skills

This phenomenon now has a name in business: “distributed deskilling”—the collective and simultaneous erosion of essential human skills within a company due to excessive or uncritical use of artificial intelligence.

(Boston Consulting Group, 2026)

0,92correlation: better human prompts, better AI responses

In another analysis of approximately 1 million conversations with Claude, Anthropic identified a correlation of over 0,92 between the educational complexity of human prompts and the AI responses they elicited.

A reminder that the stronger the person's critical capabilities, the stronger the critical quality of the AI interaction.

(Anthropic, 2026)

319workers: greater trust in AI, less critical effort

This was supported by a Microsoft study involving 319 workers.

The more people trusted AI, the less critical effort they reported. When we assume the machine knows everything, we discount our own knowledge—and even the need or possibility of challenging its answers. The study also found a shift in critical work toward verifying and integrating outputs, potentially displacing independent critique and synthesis.

(Lee et al., Microsoft/CMU, 2025)

666people: frequent AI use linked to weaker critical thinking

A particularly pressing concern for younger people, especially those aged 17 and 25 who are still forming their critical understanding of the world.

A survey and test involving 666 participants linked frequent AI use with weaker critical thinking, mediated by the outsourcing of reasoning; it raises questions about young people's ability to think beyond the machine—and the limits of its reasoning.

(Gerlich, Societies 2025)

(05)CreativityCreativity

Creative Gains, Similar Results

Creative Gains, Similar Results

Even when someone feels that the work they produce with AI is more original and creative than what they might have produced alone—which can be true—the tendency of AI outputs to converge can make the results more alike.of the time, experts adopted machine recommendations as their own

This tendency can affect even domain experts, as research on the often-invisible influence of AI agents suggests.

With strong deference to algorithmic suggestions and pressure to speed up complex analytical tasks, experts accepted the machine's choices 86% of the time and repeated its reasoning, while retaining a misleading sense of ownership over the final decision.

(Herdağdelen et al., 2026)

(06)MemoryLearning Requires Active Ownership

Learning Requires Active Ownership

What we learn—or try to understand—with AI support may be less firmly retained. We can encounter an idea and use it successfully in the moment, only to find it fades more easily when the tool is gone. That creates an illusion of learning and dependence on a synthetic system to retrieve what we thought we knew. We already live with machine-extended memory. The professional who once accumulated knowledge through doing the work risks being replaced by someone who knows how they produced a result, but not what they produced.

−17%performance after AI was removed, despite a 48% gain while using it

In an experiment involving nearly one thousand students, access to ChatGPT improved performance on mathematics exercises by 48%.

However, when the tool was removed, those same students scored 17% below peers who had never used AI.

(Bastani et al., 2025)

83,3%could not quote what they had just written with AI

An EEG study of essay writing found weaker functional brain connectivity among participants using AI;

54 students participated, and 83,3% could not quote from what they had written, compared with 11,1%who wrote without an LLM. The findings suggest a reduced sense of authorship and the phenomenon of “cognitive debt”: less of what is read—or “created”—remains with us.

(Kosmyna et al., MIT, 2025)

(07)Decision-makingThe Machine Always Knows

The Machine Always Knows

Byung-Chul Han's concept of “datacracy” describes a society that increasingly assumes answers are contained in data and that humans cannot outperform sufficiently advanced intelligent systems when faced with a complex world. This belief can carry over into human–machine interactions. We may feel that we are deciding, while simply following the direction the machine has indicated. We become AI's physical agents in the world—and even for techno-optimists, that should not be taken as praise.

44% → 3%willingness to say “I don't know” fell with AI assistance, while confidence increased

Across five experiments involving 3.132 participants, researchers selected questions on which the AI was deliberately wrong and gave its suggestions to participants.

In one replication, the willingness to answer “I don't know” fell from 44% to 3% with AI support. Despite the incorrect advice, human accuracy dropped while confidence rose. People were less accurate, yet 2,5x more confident in their answers.

(Marcoccia et al., 2026)

(01)OriginalityThe Emergence of Synthetic and Hybrid Culture

The Emergence of Synthetic and Hybrid Culture

Combining different books requires the group to create something no single book says on its own—something that does not exist anywhere else. That context emerges from the group and becomes a source of organizational originality.

3×more likely to reach top ideas when teams work with AI together

AI performs better when working from ideas developed collectively, rather than replacing each individual person's thinking.

In a field experiment involving 791 Procter & Gamble professionals, Harvard and Wharton researchers compared individuals and teams, with and without AI, while developing real product ideas. Among the top 10% of ideas by quality, teams using AI were three times more likely to generate these ideas than individuals working alone without AI. With AI, proposals also became more likely to integrate technical and commercial perspectives, crossing functional silos. The club follows the same principle: the original context emerges from the group, and that is what differentiates what AI produces in return.

(Dell’Acqua et al., 2025)

(02)DifferentiationMoving Beyond Average Thinking

Moving Beyond Average Thinking

Because most companies use the same AI systems, trained on similar knowledge bases, their outputs tend to converge—leaving less room for debate and distinctive critical or creative thinking.

dialecticshow context improves AI reasoning

We can teach AI different ways of developing ideas by providing thinking frameworks as part of its context.

Microsoft researchers found that guiding language models with a method inspired by Hegelian dialectics—developing ideas, examining contradictions, and building syntheses—could improve performance on reasoning tasks. This opens the door to original contexts in which our own ways of thinking guide how AI interprets, questions, and develops responses.

(Abdali et al., 2025)

dual focus

dual focus: the human and the machine

Why AI at work calls for renewed attention to the human mind.

This program does not reject artificial intelligence. It recognizes that, just as the shift toward office and computer work changed our physical needs—creating greater awareness of ergonomics and sedentary time— outsourcing critical and creative work calls for new adaptive structures so that we do not lose our mental abilitiesand the qualities that make us human: the capacity to imagine what could and should come next.

note

Neuroplasticityis the nervous system's ongoing ability to reorganize its structure, functions, and connections in response to experiences, learning, stimulation, or injury. If we stop asking our brains to read, reflect, connect, question, synthesize, write, imagine, and prototype, which abilities begin to lose their strength? And what might we lose while believing we are gaining something?

Artificial intelligence expands our intellectual capabilities while replacing precisely the processes through which we develop those capabilities.

Join the Club.

Better answers with AI do not necessarily mean more learning—or even better deliverables. Dependence on ready-made answers can undermine our ability to perform without the tools, persist through difficult tasks, and exercise independent judgment.

Repeated, uncritical AI use may affect our ability to think critically and creatively in the world. It may also diminish our ability to change that world in ways that reflect our own perspectives.

If, in the short term, the question cannot realistically be whether to use these tools, then it must become which human abilities we are no longer exercising while producing with them—and how to reconnect and strengthen those pathways.

(Bastani et al., 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Herdağdelen et al., 2026).

Temporal Artist · creator of the Context Club methodology

Maia Oazem studies what happens to human thinking when AI becomes part of work. She created Context Club so teams could read, discuss, and write together again—and turn their thinking into context for machines.

Born in 1990, Maia grew up between dial-up internet and mobile phones. Today she researches how the mind is changing in the post-AI era. For fifteen years, she helped build innovation and futures practices at Oracle, Salesforce, Google Cloud, and Capgemini. At Capgemini she created Papillon, a post-AI transformation initiative, as well as the research projects Post-AI Futures and Futures of Finance.

Her work has been featured by Veja, Época Negócios, Estadão, and CNBC, and presented at Febraban Tech. She teaches futures and applied AI at ESPM and serves on the board of the Brazilian Futures Association. Since 2016, she has led gira., a futures and creative strategy lab where the club's method and its collection of more than 100 books originated.
